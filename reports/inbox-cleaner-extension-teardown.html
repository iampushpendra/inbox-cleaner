Inbox cleaners compete on trustworthy decisions

Research date: 17 October 2026. The strongest established Chrome-extension competitors found are Trimbox, InboxPurge and Gmail Unsubscribe, while the closest architectural benchmarks for this repository are Gmail Cleanup and Gmail One-Click Cleaner. The market already offers local processing, sender grouping, bulk deletion and no additional sign-in, so these capabilities alone will not make Inbox Cleaner stand out. The clearest opportunity is a simpler, more trustworthy decision flow: identify the active Gmail account, explain scan coverage, show reliable sender counts, let users inspect the messages, name the exact action and provide useful recovery guidance. Competitors often blur unsubscribe, future-mail filtering and historical deletion; accurate language can become a real product advantage. This report covers 26 identified competing extension identities, a legacy Mailstrom listing, five adjacent services and Gmail's own subscription manager, with deeper analysis of seven especially relevant extensions. It evaluates public screenshots, official documentation and local source code; no extension was installed, no Google account was connected, and no mailbox action was performed.

The market spans three jobs that should stay distinct

People searching for an inbox cleaner are usually combining three different needs: remove old messages, stop unwanted future messages, and maintain order automatically. The best product for one job can be a poor fit for another. Historical cleanup benefits from volume grouping and an understandable review step. Stopping future mail requires a sender unsubscribe request or a filtering/blocking rule. Recurring organization requires persistent rules, background processing or repeated local execution. Calling all three “cleaning” conceals important differences in permissions, recovery and expectations.

The distinction matters immediately for this repository. The current extension discovers senders through Gmail's five category searches and moves selected senders' mail to Trash. It does not currently offer a verified sender-unsubscribe workflow. Its separate browser application uses Google Identity Services and the Gmail API, while the extension uses Gmail-page automation without a separate OAuth connection. That split is documented in the repository and must remain visible in product copy: the extension's no-OAuth promise should not be applied indiscriminately to the browser application. (Local README, extension manifest, popup implementation)

Gmail itself is an important baseline. Google's subscription manager presents frequent senders, recent counts, sender drilldown and unsubscribe requests. Its help page describes More → Manage subscriptions → Unsubscribe, warns about gradual availability and explains that some subscriptions require visiting the sender's website. Clicking a sender's name or avatar opens its messages. Google also distinguishes blocking, which sends future mail to Spam. A competing extension therefore needs to deliver more than a list of newsletters: historical volume, several-sender cleanup, meaningful safety controls or clearer review are stronger differentiators. (Google announcement, current Gmail Help)

How to read the evidence

Screenshot-observed means a researcher inspected pixels in an official marketing or support image. It does not mean the current installed product was exercised. Vendor-documented means an official listing, help page or website describes the behavior. Source-observed refers to local repository code, without reproducing the interaction in a real mailbox. Reviewer inference is an assessment of likely clarity or friction, not an empirical usability result. Authenticated consent screens, actual scan times, unsubscribe success, account switching, cancellation, restoration and accessibility remain untested unless explicitly stated otherwise.

User counts and star ratings below are retrieved public snapshots, sometimes from cached or localized pages. They establish approximate distribution and whether a product has a meaningful public footprint; they do not establish active users, market share, safety or task success. Conflicting snapshots are retained as ranges or flagged. Documentation and images are not guaranteed to match the same release. Pricing is likewise a dated public-source observation rather than a checkout quote.

Discovery used repeated Chrome Web Store searches for Gmail cleaner, unsubscribe, bulk delete, email cleaner and related phrases, followed by official listing and publisher checks. Products were deduplicated by extension ID rather than generic names. This is a broad discovery set, not a claim to have found every extension. Regional search, renamed listings, indexing gaps and removals make an exhaustive denominator unavailable. Four unrelated products called “Gmail Cleaner” illustrate why name-only comparisons are unreliable.

Seven extensions reveal the strongest onboarding and interaction patterns

Trimbox makes the action contextual but underspecifies an important verb

Trimbox's defining interaction is its insertion of a small aqua scissors button into Gmail's existing message list. The official screenshot places it between sender and subject, with a large annotated arrow explaining where to click. The inbox in that image is stylized with placeholder content, so it supports an assessment of placement and visual concept rather than real-world content density or hit-target measurements. The key advantage is contextual discovery: a user encountering unwanted mail can act where the annoyance occurs, without opening a separate analytics workspace. (Official screenshot, store)

Evidence: official Chrome Web Store marketing screenshot, visually inspected. This is a stylized inbox illustration, not an installed-product test. The aqua control is easy to spot, but its exact consequence requires learning.

The documented onboarding map is discover/download → install extension → connect or sign into the relevant Trimbox surface → open Gmail → use the contextual control → encounter free-plan limits or upgrade. The public website uses “Get Trimbox” and “Demo”; its download page separates “Use in your inbox” and “Install extension” from web and mobile options. Support separately documents one-time-code or magic-link login, so it would be inaccurate to claim a single observed OAuth sequence across all platforms. The website's invitation field also introduces an email-based acquisition route alongside installation. (Homepage, download options, login documentation)

Documented capabilities include mailing-list unsubscribe, deletion of historical messages from a sender, spam blocking, unlimited connected Gmail accounts and AI drafting. The public screenshots show “Auto-draft with Trimbox AI ✨” in Gmail compose, making clear that the product has expanded beyond cleaning. This breadth can increase value for existing users, but it dilutes a first impression focused on deleting newsletter clutter. The support material states a free limit of 10 mailing-list unsubscribes, with Unlimited adding unlimited unsubscribe and bulk deletion. A reliable current dollar price was not verified; upgrade terms should therefore be compared at the capability level. (Free-plan support, store)

The most useful competitive detail is its meaning of “bulk.” Support says processing is sender by sender, rather than selecting all senders for a simultaneous mass deletion. It also describes “Keep” as deleting past mail without unsubscribing. That verb is difficult to predict: a novice can reasonably interpret Keep as preserving existing messages. Inbox Cleaner already has a clearer opportunity through checkbox-based multi-sender selection and an outcome-specific Trash action. It should compare that concrete interaction rather than advertise a vague “bulk” advantage. (Mass-delete support)

Reviewer assessment: contextual placement and restrained branding are strong; an unlabeled scissors action needs first-use explanation, and “Keep” is a semantic weakness. No tooltip, confirmation, keyboard behavior or successful unsubscribe was observed. Privacy and certification statements remain vendor assertions rather than an independent audit. The transferable pattern is proximity to the email, not the unexplained icon or the enlarged feature suite.

InboxPurge presents the clearest evidenced multi-sender workspace

InboxPurge combines a Gmail-side panel with sender search, checkboxes, counts and separately visible “Unsubscribe” and “Delete” controls. Its purple unsubscribe action and red deletion action help establish distinct consequences. Account information appears at the top, followed by a “Subscriptions” destination and “Search your subscriptions.” This gives users a recognizable sequence: find sender, inspect volume, select rows, choose an operation. The sidebar keeps Gmail visible, although it sacrifices space for long sender addresses and adds another navigation layer. (Official screenshot)

Evidence: official store screenshot, visually inspected. Account context, search, counts, selection and action choice share one workspace. Current installed behavior was not tested.

Its public post-install page is particularly useful. It states “Installed & ready” and offers “Open Gmail & Clean My Inbox”, with a claim that connection takes about 30 seconds. This is a good bridge between successful installation and a user knowing where to begin. The documented map is install → welcome page → open Gmail → connect → scan subscriptions → select senders → unsubscribe or delete → confirm. The page's duration is a vendor estimate, not measured onboarding time. Its reassurance about local data does not mean no OAuth: the privacy policy documents consented Gmail API access and local handling. (Welcome page, privacy policy)

The confirmation screenshot shows a numeric total, a generic “Confirm” button and an “Advanced Deletion Options” disclosure. “Exclude Important Emails” is checked, while “Exclude Starred Emails” is unchecked in the inspected image. The surrounding promotional caption claims both important and starred messages stay untouched, which overstates the depicted defaults. This is not evidence of a reproduced deletion bug; it is a concrete inconsistency between screenshot and marketing. It demonstrates why protection policies must be visible in the actual review surface and accurately described. “Move 1,238 emails to Trash” would communicate more than “Confirm.” (Confirmation screenshot)

Beyond deletion, the documented product offers mass unsubscribe, a topic-based newsletter digest, sender unblocking and Gmail action-bar integration. Its public plan explanations distinguish free access, a seven-day BASIC period and licensed PRO, with one license for unlimited Gmail accounts and a 14-day refund. Exact current price and free-action quotas were not reliably exposed. Older testimonials mentioning a price should not be treated as a live offer. Website adoption claims also lag the store snapshot. (Homepage and FAQ, store)

Reviewer assessment: this is the strongest evidenced reference for Inbox Cleaner's multi-sender interaction. The account identity, visible counts and distinct actions are worth adapting. The narrow workspace, generic confirmation and hidden safety settings are weaknesses to avoid. Its ratings support prioritizing it for competitive research, not declaring its flow empirically superior.

Gmail Unsubscribe makes progress and completion understandable

Gmail Unsubscribe, published by Baxter, provides the clearest public example of a staged cleanup journey. The official cleanup screenshot has a lavender-and-white panel, visible connected account and a Connected → Clean up → Done stepper. Sender checkboxes and counts sit on the left; the right panel summarizes selection and offers Archive and Trash tiles. “Still searchable” explains Archive, while “Gone in 30 days” explains Trash. A large purple selection-aware action and subdued Skip link establish a strong next step without eliminating the ability to leave. (Cleanup screenshot)

Evidence: official product screenshot, visually inspected. The stepper and outcome descriptions improve predictability. The image also preselects three senders, a questionable default for consequential actions.

The documented map is install → open toolbar → scan → review grouped subscriptions → select senders and historical-mail treatment → unsubscribe plus archive/Trash → see completion → return to Gmail. The success screenshot reinforces closure with a green check, sender total, archived-message number and “Go to Gmail.” This is more helpful than merely closing a dialog because it tells users what happened and where to resume work. Per-sender statuses and fallback unsubscribe methods are documented, though execution was not tested. (Website, success screenshot)

Two concerns complicate an otherwise strong interaction. First, the screenshot states that three noisy senders were preselected and can be unticked. Preselection can accelerate a demonstration but weakens consent when the next step unsubscribes or moves historical mail. Second, the primary action emphasizes unsubscribe while the workflow also archives or trashes mail. The resulting side effect should be included in the final action label or immediately adjacent summary. A denominator referring to recent messages is also insufficient if the action affects all sender history: discovery scope and action scope need separate explanation.

The official sources directly conflict on product limits. The website describes a newest-three-month discovery window, 25 free senders per month and Pro at $35.88 per year. The store describes full-history scanning, five free unsubscribes per month and free bulk archive/delete. These are material differences in both value and expectation; neither should be presented as the settled current entitlement without checking the installed product and checkout. Its Undo is documented as restoring moved messages, not reversing an unsubscribe request. (Website, store)

Reviewer assessment: strong hierarchy, account visibility, progress and completion make this a useful onboarding benchmark. The experience is less trustworthy where selection defaults, combined actions and inconsistent quotas obscure consequences. The lesson is to borrow the explicit stages and outcome explanations while keeping selection deliberate and product terms consistent.

Gmail Cleanup proves that a small no-OAuth product can be a serious comparator

Gmail Cleanup has a much smaller public footprint than Trimbox or InboxPurge, but is strategically close to the local product. It adds “Delete all from…” to Gmail's toolbar, works without a Google account connection and is documented as free. A user selects one message or up to 100 senders, reviews the total, confirms and moves matching messages to Trash. Its promise is narrow enough to understand without learning a new dashboard. (Store, official FAQ)

The documented map is install → open Gmail → select a message or sender set → choose the sender-specific toolbar action → review affected total → confirm → wait for Trash movement. Official illustrations use charcoal backgrounds, white and yellow headlines and a yellow “Delete all from everdeals.com” pill pointing to the toolbar. These are conceptual visuals, so they demonstrate communication and intended placement more convincingly than real modal quality. The sender name inside the action is a particularly good affordance: it reduces ambiguity about the meaning of “all.”

Its simplicity comes with a clear limitation: documented cleanup does not exclude starred or unread messages. Users seeking protective rules need to know that before confirming. Its FAQ also candidly says hundreds of messages can take more than a minute and thousands can take several minutes. That is a useful benchmark for local Gmail-page automation: an honest expectation is preferable to promising instant cleanup. No independent timing measurement was performed. (Official FAQ)

Reviewer assessment: strong scope language and minimal onboarding make this a more relevant reference than its small user count suggests. It prevents Inbox Cleaner from claiming that “no OAuth” or “free bulk cleanup” is unique. Inbox Cleaner needs to add value through sender discovery, trustworthy grouping and review, while preserving the same simplicity of action.

Gmail One-Click Cleaner offers deep safety controls inside a crowded surface

Gmail One-Click Cleaner is unusually feature-rich for a local-processing competitor. Its listing documents a read-only Mailbox Report, sender census, storage analysis, suggestions, age/size/category/search rules, preset depths, custom rules, scheduling, dry-run/review/live modes, protection controls and a Recovery Log with Restore. It also documents sender triage and checking unsubscribe outcomes after two weeks. Those are meaningful patterns for consent and recovery, although they remain publisher claims rather than independently exercised behavior. (Store)

The official screenshot has a dark popup with cyan “RUN CLEANER”, a recommended “Monthly Light Clean,” age controls and toggles for Promotions, Social & updates, Big attachments and No-reply. “Clean,” “Unsubscribe” and “Storage” form one navigation layer, while Rules, Stats, Diagnostics, version and Pro form another. An Auto-Pilot Pro section adds more competing information. The main button is visually prominent, but “RUN CLEANER” is less specific than the operation it triggers. A novice still has to understand the selected preset, toggles and mode to predict the result. (Official screenshot)

Evidence: official store screenshot, visually inspected; release match unverified. Strong contrast of the primary action coexists with considerable navigation and settings density.

The documented map is install → open Gmail → use “Clean Gmail” entry → read-only report → choose recommended action, triage or rules → review/dry run → execute with progress → inspect Recovery Log → restore if needed. This model delivers value before asking for destructive trust. Its strongest concept is using the same safety filters for preview counts and actual actions, which prevents a reassuring small count from preceding a wider destructive operation. This is directly relevant to Inbox Cleaner's current category-discovery versus All Mail action scope.

The listing presents free cleanup, rules, scheduling, dry run and restore, three free unsubscribes and $9.99 one-time Pro. That is a documented price snapshot rather than verified checkout. Retrieved version and rating snapshots varied between crawls, reinforcing that release-specific claims need care. Local processing and Chrome's disclosure that communications, website content or personal information are handled are not inherently contradictory: data can be read locally without being transmitted to the vendor. Neither statement amounts to a network audit. (Store)

The completion screenshot says “Cleanup Complete!” and explains movement to Trash, but promotional framing about storage freed risks confusing cleanup with quota reclamation. It also foregrounds sharing and rating. A cleaner's first completion priority should be a trustworthy receipt, review and recovery; promotional requests become appropriate after the user can verify the result. Reviewer assessment: best-in-set documented safety breadth, with a significant cognitive-density cost. Borrow report-first, preview and reliable restoration concepts rather than copying the entire control surface.

Tidy Tiger makes actions approachable but blurs future-mail semantics

Tidy Tiger combines an extension for daily Gmail maintenance with a web Smart Scan for deeper cleanup. Its website distinguishes “Start Guided Scan” from “Add to Gmail,” and describes a web journey of Connect Gmail → Lean Back → Remove in Minutes. The extension's documented path is install → open a message → click the tiger → choose Unsubscribe, Delete one or Delete all. This contextual model is easy to explain, but the two product surfaces create a choice that onboarding must resolve. (Website, store)

The inspected Smart Scan screenshot shows generous white rows, sender identity, total and unread count/percentage, a large pale-purple outlined “Unsubscribe & Delete All” action and a chevron for alternatives. It is approachable and explicit about doing two things, although “All” still requires sender and historical scope. Repeating a large primary button on every row can make the list visually busy and may be less efficient than a batch-selection bar for many senders. This is an interaction hypothesis, not measured comparative speed. Crucially, this screenshot is the web dashboard, not verified extension UI. (Official image)

Evidence: official web Smart Scan screenshot, visually inspected. It supports an assessment of the web dashboard's row hierarchy; it does not establish the appearance of the extension.

The largest issue is semantic. The vendor FAQ explains its “unsubscribe” behavior as filtering future messages directly to Trash. That can keep the inbox quiet, but it is not the same operation as asking the sender to remove an address from its mailing list. It also creates continuing Trash accumulation and recovery implications. The store documents local processing and a free start without a card, but exact current paid pricing was not established. (FAQ, store)

Reviewer assessment: strong friendliness, large visible actions and useful unread context; less strong conceptual precision. The mascot demonstrates that consequential tasks need not feel intimidating. Inbox Cleaner can use a similarly approachable tone while naming future filtering “Block future mail” or “Move future mail to Trash” if such a feature is ever implemented.

Email Cleaner makes retention rules tangible but asks for early trust

Email Cleaner approaches the job through ongoing rules rather than a single cleanup session. Its official listing documents install → Google sign-in → choose a suggested or custom rule → ongoing processing. Rules include older-than-N-days and keep the latest N messages, with delete, archive or mark-read actions. It also describes domain grouping, exact previews, noisiest-sender suggestions, editable/pausable/removable rules, history restore and ongoing statistics. Background operation without an open Gmail tab is part of its documented proposition. (Store)

“Keep the latest N” is a particularly concrete mental model: preserve a small recent set rather than understand dates and search syntax. It is a useful future direction for receipts, notifications and recurring newsletters. However, asking for a recurring rule before the first visible cleanup means users must trust future behavior before seeing present accuracy. A safer introduction would show a one-off preview, demonstrate the result and then offer to repeat the same policy. That is a proposed improvement, not an observed product flow.

The listing's “delete” and restore terms do not establish whether all cases mean Trash, permanent deletion or a constrained reversal window. That gap should remain visible rather than filling a feature matrix with confident safety claims. Free core functionality and an upgrade are documented, but a reliable current price was not established. Cached users and ratings conflicted. No current product screenshot was inspected, so visual polish, typography, button hierarchy and overall design quality are unassessed. The review can judge the documented interaction model without inventing an appearance score.

Smaller products contain useful patterns despite limited distribution

Unsub explicitly describes a two-sender trial, then “All remaining,” “Start,” Pause/Stop and a running log. It depends on native Gmail Manage subscriptions and checks availability. This is valuable for Inbox Cleaner's architecture: a tiny, observable trial can establish confidence in browser automation before a long run. MailDefender documents Passed, Needs manual step and Failed outcomes, opening unsubscribe pages three at a time. Those states are more honest than reporting every attempted request as success. (Unsub listing, MailDefender listing)

Bulk Delete Emails for Gmail documents a read-only dry run, cancellation, export, recent action history and a separately gated permanent-delete mode requiring typed confirmation and permission. These are interesting safeguards, but its tiny footprint and absence of execution evidence mean they should be treated as ideas to validate, not established reliability. The open repository for Gmail Mass Unsubscribe explicitly admits expired links and browser-origin rejection of some unsubscribe endpoints, a useful corrective to universal-success marketing. (Bulk Delete listing, Gmail Mass Unsubscribe limitations)

A transparent qualitative rubric avoids false usability precision

A design teardown can identify a weak label without measuring a conversion rate. It cannot infer a task-success percentage from a marketing screenshot. The rubric here therefore uses strong, mixed, weak and unassessed, not a pseudo-scientific overall score. Entry clarity asks whether the next step is explicit. Action clarity asks whether the verb, target and consequence are predictable. Scope/safety asks whether counts, exclusions and recovery are explained. Visual hierarchy asks whether a screenshot communicates a primary path. These are reviewer judgments; evidence confidence describes how much of the flow was actually visible.

The words unsubscribe, block, archive and Trash must remain distinct

A genuine unsubscribe request asks a sender to stop a mailing list. A filter-to-Trash rule changes what Gmail does with arriving mail. Blocking generally redirects future sender mail to Spam. Archiving removes the Inbox label while keeping messages searchable. Moving to Trash puts existing mail into a recoverable location until permanent deletion. These actions differ in what can be reversed and in whether future messages continue to arrive. Gmail's own subscription help distinguishes unsubscribe from blocking; Tidy Tiger and Cleanfox documentation demonstrate why a vendor's marketing verb requires checking against its actual described behavior. (Gmail subscription help, Tidy Tiger FAQ, Cleanfox FAQ)

For Inbox Cleaner, the current outcome should consistently be described as moving existing mail to Trash. “Delete them” is understandable shorthand only when the review surface clarifies recoverability and scope. A prominent “Unsubscribe” button would be misleading until a real sender-request or explicitly named alternative is implemented. Likewise, restoring trashed messages does not resubscribe a user. Baxter's documentation makes this distinction explicit, an example worth following. (Gmail Unsubscribe)

The quota distinction also matters. Moving mail to Trash does not immediately reclaim Gmail storage. Storage messaging needs to distinguish mail moved out of the working mailbox from mail permanently removed and quota recalculated. A count of cleaned messages is defensible after verifying the movement; a claimed number of bytes freed is a different measurement. Gmail's standard Trash lifecycle is 30 days, while permanent deletion removes the recovery path. Those consequences belong near the action when users are cleaning specifically to recover storage. (Google storage guidance, Gmail deletion and recovery)

Adjacent services raise the ceiling while introducing different costs

Clean Email offers a broad management suite rather than a verified extension

Clean Email's official surfaces describe web/cloud, iOS, Android and Mac delivery. The research did not verify an official Chrome extension identity, so it should be compared as a substitute for the same job rather than mislabeled. It offers smart folders, grouping, suggestions, archive/move/label/Trash, unsubscribe, Read Later, Pause, Keep Newest, Auto Clean and Screener. Its greater breadth is meaningful for long-term organization; it also creates more concepts for a new user to understand. (Official homepage)

Its documented journey is start free → connect provider → load/group mail → inspect group subjects → choose cleanup or unsubscribe policy → confirm → optionally automate. The Unsubscriber guide describes a toggle for trashing existing messages that is on by default, and a confirmation-suppression option whose risk the documentation acknowledges. That is a useful consent lesson: future-mail prevention and existing-message removal deserve distinct controls even when a combined workflow is convenient. Current visual quality was not assessed from inspected screenshots. (Unsubscriber guide, bulk flow)

The free allowance is documented as 1,000 cleaning actions/messages and 25 unsubscribes, alongside a 14-day premium trial without a card; paid upgrade can charge immediately during that trial. Plans cover 1, 5 or 10 accounts, but dynamic current dollar prices did not render reliably. The vendor says it processes headers/metadata, does not download bodies or attachments and stores metadata for 45 days. These are cloud-service claims, not evidence that everything stays on-device. (Trial documentation, plans, homepage)

Leave Me Alone shows a friendly, legible alternative to dense dashboards

Leave Me Alone's inspected support screenshot presents a pale page, envelope mascot and a spacious card reading “Let's connect your first email account!” A single email field is the main task, with guidance to enter an address to see further options. That is low visual complexity, although the helper does not fully explain which permission step comes next. Its documented Gmail flow continues through “Connect to Google” and permission review. The guide openly explains broad reading/composing/sending and settings permissions, which is useful preparation for an otherwise alarming consent screen. (Connection guide, Gmail guide)

Evidence: official annotated support screenshot, visually inspected. Friendly framing and a single field reduce visual load; it does not show the subsequent authenticated permission flow.

A second inspected sender-row image pairs sender name/address and “12 monthly” with named “Keep in Inbox,” “Add to Rollup” and “Unsubscribe” pills. The frequency qualifier makes the count's time scope clearer than a bare number. Equal emphasis gives users neutral choices, while Rollup still needs a nearby explanation. This wide row is a useful hierarchy reference rather than a layout to copy into a 400-pixel popup. (Official sender-row image, workflow guide)

Its documented scan guidance is unusually candid: most scans take minutes; scanning can continue in the background after leaving the page; by default it searches mailing-list messages from the previous six months in Inbox and user folders, excluding Spam/Trash unless opted in. Inbox Cleaner should emulate this precision about time and coverage while describing its own different operating constraints. It must not inherit the cloud product's promise that work continues with Gmail closed. (Scanning guide)

Leave Me Alone also offers actual unsubscribe requests, rollups, Screener, Spam Blocker, priority senders, DND and multiple accounts. Ten free unsubscribes are documented; a Seven Day Pass description says $19 for two accounts and seven days, but contradictory dynamic zero-dollar cards mean checkout pricing remains unverified. Its privacy policy is feature-dependent: unsubscribe-only metadata differs from rollup content and optional spam-classification bodies/training samples. “Never reads email content” would be an incorrect global summary, even though the service states no user-data sale. (Homepage, August 2026 privacy policy)

Unroll.Me and Cleanfox change the meaning of free

Unroll.Me officially describes web and mobile apps, with Keep, Block and Rollup choices. Its desktop Gmail instructions enumerate a long provider-specific connection path including data consent, two-factor authentication and an app password. This is documented friction, not a reproduced completion time, and the vendor recommends mobile as easier for Gmail connection. Work or school account constraints can add uncertainty. Its main product advantage is recurring blocking/digest organization rather than simply bulk trashing historical messages. (Devices, Gmail connection, homepage)

The service is free to users, but its current business explanation says commercial-email information and demographic details support measurement, advertising products and datasets sold to businesses or data brokers. This deserves clear comparison when local privacy is a purchase criterion. It should be described from current policy rather than reduced to older “anonymized only” marketing. No current product screenshot was inspected, so the report does not score its visual design. (Cost FAQ, data explanation, privacy)

Cleanfox documents keep, delete-existing and automatically-delete-existing-and-future choices. Its older official interaction guide describes swiping in three directions and a list with red/orange/green states, sender received totals and opened totals. That maps neatly to a small decision set, but gestures and colors require learning; explicit text actions would make the same model more predictable on desktop. Current appearance and live onboarding were not verified, so this is a critique of documented interaction semantics. (How it works, FAQ)

Cleanfox's free model includes commercial statistics derived from newsletters and transactions and NielsenIQ consumer-panel membership. Its privacy text has Gmail-specific use restrictions and transfer provisions, so “all Gmail data is sold” would be an unsupported oversimplification. The comparison should explain the documented commercial model and let users evaluate it. Neither Cleanfox nor Unroll.Me was verified as an official current Chrome extension in this research. (English site, privacy)

Mailstrom and SaneBox trade local simplicity for sustained organization

Mailstrom is currently a connected web client supporting Gmail, Outlook, AOL, iCloud and IMAP. Its grouping facets include Senders, People I've emailed, Subjects, Mailing Lists, Time, Social, Shopping and Size. The inspected official image shows strong selected navigation and sender search, suggesting a powerful refinement model; it does not show a complete action workflow. Its signup interstitial explains the next Google permission checkbox and labels the action “Signup with Google.” That permission preparation is good, though its broad “We Don't Store Emails” reassurance needs the FAQ's more precise statement that metadata is stored on servers and content is accessible for display. (FAQ, signup)

Documented features include delete/archive/move, unsubscribe, block sender/subject, automatic rules, Undo/history, Chill and Expire. The current pricing page lists Basic $9/month or $59.95/year, Plus $14/month or $99.95/year and Pro $29.95/month or $199.95/year, with differing account and message limits. The FAQ trial loads the latest 5,000 messages and permits action on 25%; a March 2026 marketing article instead says a 14-day full-access trial. That conflict should be checked before quoting a trial promise. (Pricing, FAQ, marketing article)

SaneBox works within existing mail clients without a download, providing importance filtering, SaneLater, SaneNews, BlackHole, Deep Clean, reminders and digest. Its strength is continuing organization; its branded vocabulary adds learning cost compared with ordinary verbs. The documented trial is seven days. Plan tiers vary accounts and enabled features, while the publicly retrieved dollar figures were a biyearly billing offer, not standard monthly prices. Those promotional equivalents should not be copied into a simple monthly comparison. (Homepage, tour, plan explanation, specific pricing variant)

SaneBox describes header analysis and optional body retrieval without storing those bodies, encrypted credentials and security certification claims. These should be evaluated as service architecture and vendor commitments, not equated with local-only processing. Across these services the commercial tradeoff is consistent: connected processing can continue while the browser is closed and provide broader organization, while a local extension can reduce account-connection friction and keep a narrower interaction easier to explain. (Security)

Inbox Cleaner should prioritize account certainty before adding features

The local comparison is based on source inspection, not rendered UI or a reproduced mailbox incident. The popup is a compact 400×580 interface using system typography, light surfaces, sender metadata, count chips, category badges and a sticky selected-action bar. The opening state has “Scan my inbox,” while results provide “Clear” and “Move to Trash.” These are already more focused than a full cloud suite. The largest opportunities are about trust and coherence rather than importing every feature in the inventory. (Popup markup, styles, logic)

Priority 0: bind the scan, cache and action to the same account

The observed findGmailTabId() favors the active Gmail tab and otherwise takes the first Gmail tab. Cached data uses a global inboxCleanerData key without an account identifier in the inspected payload. The header presents counts and timing rather than an email address, and cached results can appear even when the content script is unavailable. Together these facts create an account/cache trust risk. No wrong-account deletion was reproduced, so this must not be reported as an established incident. It is nevertheless more consequential than adding unsubscribe or a prettier chart. (Popup logic, content script)

The proposed behavior is to show the active address before scanning and before action, namespace the cache by account and bind the results to the Gmail tab/account that generated them. A mismatch should require a fresh scan or explicit account resolution. Stale results should remain visibly stale rather than appearing ready for action. InboxPurge and Baxter demonstrate how little space account context needs; it is a trust cue that belongs in the primary hierarchy.

Acceptance should be tested with two Gmail accounts, active-tab changes, popup reopening and disconnected content scripts. The user should always be able to answer “Which account am I cleaning?” without inferring it from the browser behind the popup. Those checks would validate a safety property that screenshots alone cannot establish.

Priority 0: make the selected total and action scope truthful everywhere

The README explains five-category sender discovery, exact All Mail counts for the top 250 senders and approximate counts for the remaining long tail. Rows indicate approximation, while the inspected updateActionBar() sums stored counts and prints a plain total even if a selected row is approximate. The confirmation is more careful, using “at least” when needed and explaining archived-mail inclusion. This creates a real source-observed copy inconsistency, though the full interaction was not exercised. (README limitations, popup logic, parsing helpers)

The fix should be conceptual before visual: choose consistent entities and scope. Gmail rows can represent conversations; a sender total must not silently switch between conversations and messages. Category badges describe discovery, while a sender query can affect archived mail too. Every selected count, confirmation and receipt should reflect that same unit and scope. If an approximate selection cannot be pre-counted accurately, the action bar must say “at least” or “estimated,” and the final review must make the broader query explicit.

A clear action could read “Move 1,238 emails to Trash” only when both the count and entity are verified. Otherwise use a truthful count qualifier and explicit scope. Provide a sender-level “View messages in Gmail” route and a batch preview so users can inspect what will be affected. Do not let a Promotions badge imply Promotions-only deletion. One-Click Cleaner's documented same-filter preview/action approach and Baxter's recent-scan/all-history caveat make this the most important cross-product lesson.

Priority 1: turn the first scan into an understandable commitment

The current opening copy says “across every category — then delete them” and has no compact timing/privacy/safety preflight. The source already supports a better explanation: the extension works locally in the open Gmail session, scans five category searches and takes minutes because it paginates and verifies sender counts. Its scope is still bounded by category discovery and exact-count limits; “every email” would overpromise. (Popup markup, README)

A proposed first screen can keep one primary action, “Scan Gmail senders,” with short supporting text: “Find senders across Gmail's five categories. Usually takes several minutes. Nothing moves until you review and confirm. Runs locally in this browser.” Final wording should match the implemented operating constraints: whether Gmail must remain open, whether users can close the popup and what navigation automation will change. A local extension should never copy a cloud competitor's background promise without implementing it.

The missing-Gmail screen should provide “Open Gmail” when that action is available, followed by an appropriate reload/retry instruction. The current “Retry” alone asks the user to solve an unstated prerequisite. A useful onboarding system carries people to the next actionable context, as InboxPurge's welcome page does, rather than only describing the problem.

Priority 1: report progress and completion as useful receipts

The current scan has category and sender-count phases, a good foundation, but no visible pause/stop control in the popup. Detail is truncated, and success is reduced to “Deletion complete ✓.” The next iteration should name the phases “Discovering senders” and “Verifying counts,” show real progress information and elapsed time, and explain why Gmail is navigating. An exact ETA should appear only if it can be supported. A cancel control requires genuine cancellation behavior; a decorative button would undermine the trust it is supposed to create. (Popup implementation, content script)

A completion receipt should state verified moved, skipped and failed totals separately, identify the account and make “Open Trash” and recovery instructions easy to reach. An incomplete scan should preserve the last good results with clear labeling rather than converting partial progress into a reassuring success. MailDefender's differentiated statuses and Baxter's completion page show how status language can reduce uncertainty without adding a whole dashboard.

A true whole-run Undo is a separate implementation problem. Gmail Trash offers recoverability, but a broad sender search cannot reliably identify exactly which messages one action moved. Restoration needs persistent action identity and verified tracking; it should not be simulated with an overbroad restore query. The immediate truthful improvement is recovery guidance and an accurate receipt, followed by engineered reversal when the necessary tracking exists.

Priority 1: make protection policy visible before action

Protecting starred, important, unread or labeled mail can be valuable, but only when the selection/count/action path applies the policy consistently. InboxPurge's screenshot-caption discrepancy demonstrates the cost of promising protection that is hidden, optional or differently configured. The local product should first state whether those messages are included. If exclusions are implemented later, they must alter preview totals and actual queries together. A checkbox that looks safe but does not govern every path is worse than an honest inclusion warning.

No preselection is needed for the current sender list. Users can deliberately choose groups based on volume and context. Default selection should favor certainty, while non-destructive inspection remains easy. For high-volume deletion, a readable summary of account, scope, selected senders, count confidence and protection policy is more useful than a dramatic generic warning.

Priority 2: improve label clarity and visual accessibility without enlarging the product

“Clear selection” is more explicit than “Clear.” A text-labeled Rescan is easier to discover than relying on the refresh icon's title. Count-chip colors should be explained as volume rather than implying danger or importance. Name and email address should both remain available because display names can collide. A view-in-Gmail action can help users investigate without requiring a new message reader inside the extension. These are small improvements that preserve the existing compact model.

Source inspection suggests the dark theme uses a pale-blue accent while some primary text remains white, which merits a contrast check; no measured accessibility failure is claimed. Keyboard focus, row/checkbox semantics, dialog focus and screen-reader labels also need a rendered audit. The report's visual judgments should not substitute for those checks. The desired result is a legible list with one obvious primary action, not a miniature version of Mailstrom's full workspace. (Styles, markup, logic)

Priority 2: resolve the public product story before expanding monetization

The retrieved store description and local architecture appear out of step. Verify the published package, installed permissions, store copy and website funnel together, then state the extension and browser app models separately. A user arriving from a no-OAuth promise should not unexpectedly see API permission language, and a user examining the browser app should not be given extension-specific local-DOM claims. This is an evidence gap to resolve, not permission to claim the store package has already changed.

Any future limit or upgrade should be visible before the user invests in scanning and selecting. Trimbox's free-limit help article and Baxter's contradictory quotas show how easily pricing uncertainty contaminates onboarding trust. The product can monetize later features while keeping current cleanup language precise. Archive and age filters are plausible next additions because they support cautious cleanup within the existing mental model. Digests, AI writing and cloud classification would introduce different jobs and operating assumptions, so they should follow validated demand.

A proposed end-to-end flow keeps the strongest ideas small

The recommended target journey is open extension → verify account → read scan scope/timing → scan without mutation → inspect ranked sender results → select deliberately → preview exact or qualified scope → confirm named Trash action → see progress → review receipt and recovery. This combines InboxPurge's contextual bridge, Baxter's progress and closure, Gmail Cleanup's sender-specific wording and One-Click Cleaner's preview discipline. It remains focused on the job the local extension already performs.

A successful first usability round should ask people to explain which account is active, what the scan covered, whether archived mail is included, what an approximate number means, what will happen when they click the primary action and how to recover afterward. Record hesitation, mistaken predictions and failure recovery in a seeded account. Only after that study should “easy to understand” become a measured claim. For now, the report identifies concrete reasons a design is more or less predictable, and separates those judgments from observed behavior.

The immediate strategic position is therefore a local sender-cleanup tool that makes the scope and consequences understandable. The most durable advantage will come from accurate counts, account binding and receipts that survive edge cases. Those qualities are less conspicuous than another feature badge, but they address the moment when a user decides whether to trust a button with years of email.
